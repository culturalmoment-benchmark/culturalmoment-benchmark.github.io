What is the Cultural Moment Benchmark?

  • The idea: cultural understanding in video is not one ability but three: naming what a concept symbolizes, visually recognizing it on video, and locating its sub-events in time. CMB scores each ability separately, so a single aggregate can no longer hide the bottleneck.
  • The task: every concept runs through three stages. Stage 1 picks the concept name from four candidates given a symbolic description. Stage 2 picks among four unlabeled video moments for the same description. Stage 3 predicts free-form start and end times of a target sub-event on a different video. Each stage runs under three context modes: Reset, Carry, and Feedback.
  • Why it matters: even the strongest closed-source models get fewer than 30% of concepts right across all three stages, open-source models score in single digits, and a 14-rater human study shows the required knowledge is country-specific, not regional.

Cultural Moment Benchmark: Evaluating Video Cultural Reasoning and Grounding in Southeast Asia

One concept, three probes: Stage 1 names it, Stage 2 recognizes it among unlabeled video moments, Stage 3 localizes its sub-event on a different video.

Abstract

Cultural understanding in video means more than recognizing what is visible; it requires grasping the symbolic and temporal significance of cultural concepts. We decompose this into three abilities: naming what a concept symbolizes, visually recognizing it on video, and locating its sub-events in time. Existing video-cultural benchmarks tend to test what is seen, collapsing these three abilities into a single score that hides the bottleneck. We introduce the Cultural Moment Benchmark (CMB): 306 expert-curated concepts from seven countries in Southeast Asia across five categories. We evaluate each concept through three stages, one per ability. Given a description, Stage 1 (S1) selects from four candidate concept names, Stage 2 (S2) selects from four candidate video moments, and Stage 3 (S3) predicts the start and end times of the moment in a video. To keep each stage focused on a distinct ability, we use three design choices: semantic-similarity distractors (S1, S2), unlabeled video moments (S2), and free-form localization on a different example video (S3). Across six vision-language models, the failure mode varies by ability and modality. i) Even the strongest closed-source models score below 30% when all three stages must be correct; ii) Across stages, the three abilities do not fully cascade: naming a concept correctly helps half the models recognize it on video, but recognizing it has little effect on locating the sub-event in time; iii) Audio is complementary, redundant, or distracting depending on the concept, more often distracting in countries with non-Latin scripts; removing both audio and subtitles hurts Games and Music the most. Separately, our 14-rater human study shows that even Expert raters score below chance on concepts from a neighboring country, indicating that CMB requires country-specific cultural knowledge. CMB acts as a diagnostic harness, attributing failures to a specific ability or modality rather than aggregating them into a single score.

Key Findings

  1. End-to-end success is low for every model. Even Gemini 3.1 Pro and GPT-5.4, the two strongest models, get fewer than 30% of concepts right across all three stages. The four open-source models score in single digits on the Joint metric, and InternVL-14B is near zero.
  2. The three abilities do not fully cascade. Correctly naming a concept helps the strongest models pick the right video moment (about 18 points for Gemini and GPT), but choosing the right moment provides no measurable benefit when predicting a span on a fresh clip. Temporal localization is the floor every model shares, and purpose-built temporal-grounding models do not clear it either: the best specialist reaches 19.3 mIoU overall, versus 33.4 for the best VLM.
  3. Prior context helps the strong and hurts the weak. Under Carry and Feedback, Gemini and GPT climb by ten points and more, the Qwen models barely move, and the InternVL configurations lose accuracy.
  4. Audio is not uniformly useful. Depending on the concept, audio is complementary, redundant, or distracting. 29% of videos from non-Latin-script countries (Cambodia, Myanmar, Thailand) have audio rated as distracting, versus 14% for Latin-script countries; removing audio and subtitles together hurts Games and Music the most.
  5. Cultural knowledge is country-specific, not regional. In the 14-rater human study, Expert raters drop 37 points on S1 for a neighboring country's concepts, landing below the 25% chance level. The knowledge that helps experts at home misleads them abroad.

Dataset and Construction

CMB is built on one design principle: humans produce all visual ground truth; LLMs scaffold only textual artifacts. Local Annotators (native speakers) validate concepts and filter candidate videos; Cultural Annotators (native speakers with research or co-curricular involvement in their country's traditions) curate the symbolic descriptions and annotate S2 moments and S3 spans; non-local Reviewers run an Outsider Filter on every concept, answering each description from regional priors alone, so descriptions that leak regionally guessable cues are rewritten or discarded. DeepSeek-R1 audits option sets and sub-event descriptions as text only; it never sees video, audio, or frames.

The construction pipeline: preprocessing yields 306 concepts and 624 source videos; each concept is materialized into S1, S2, and S3 questions; every concept passes the Outsider Filter and S3 triage before entering the validated benchmark.

The 306 concepts are organized as country/category/concept over seven countries: four with Latin script (Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Vietnam) and three with non-Latin scripts (Cambodia, Myanmar, Thailand). From about 6,120 candidate videos found by concept-and-country search, Local Annotators keep 624: Set A (one per concept, 306 videos, 2.0 minutes on average) for Stage 2, and Set B (318 videos, 6.2 minutes on average) for Stage 3.

Release

All 624 source videos were publicly hosted on YouTube at annotation time. To respect the platform's terms and uploader copyright, CMB releases YouTube video IDs, S2 moment timestamps, and S3 ground-truth spans, not video bytes, together with the full evaluation suite: inference wrappers for all six models, scoring for all stages and modes including c-S2, c-mIoU, and Joint, the cluster-bootstrap script behind every confidence interval, and the scored per-pair records of every model on every concept.

The benchmark items are released in stages: a stratified public sample covering all seven countries and five categories arrives first on HuggingFace for development, while the remaining items are held back as a hidden test set for a planned shared task. Releases are versioned so evaluations can be tied to a specific snapshot, and an opt-out protocol covers uploaders whose videos are referenced. Annotations and the evaluation suite are released under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 for non-commercial research.

Task definition

Input: a symbolic description of a cultural concept (S1, S2), four candidate names (S1) or four unlabeled candidate video moments (S2), and a different full video with a target sub-event description (S3). Output: the chosen option (S1, S2) and a free-form [tstart, tend] span (S3). Metrics: accuracy for S1 and S2, mean IoU for S3, the conditional metrics c-S2 and c-mIoU for cascade analysis, and the Joint score for end-to-end success. A stratified public sample is available for development; the hidden test set is reserved for the planned shared task.

What's next

CMB covers seven SEA countries and five categories; other SEA countries (Brunei, Laos, Timor-Leste, Singapore) and further categories are out of scope for now, reflecting the availability of qualified Local Annotators and high-quality source videos. We hope the CMB framework serves as a template for broadening cultural assessment to other underrepresented regions. If you want to collaborate on culturally aware multimodal AI or extend the framework to new regions, book a chat or email buraks@smu.edu.sg.
